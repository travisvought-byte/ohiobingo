Change Ohio's bingo law

Pay the people who run the game.

A weekly bingo session takes about six hours, every week of the year, plus separate trips to count money, make deposits, keep records and order supplies. Ohio law says the people who do that work can't be paid a cent. That's a big reason community games across Ohio are closing.

Why this matters

For many seniors, veterans and people in long-term care, a weekly game is the main reason to leave the building and see friends. Traditional bingo can't run without a crew, and the law says that crew has to work for free.

So the game only survives where an organization has retirees who can give hundreds of unpaid hours a year. Anyone who needs income to live is shut out of serving their own community this way. When the volunteers age out, the game closes.

What the work really takes

Six hours a session, one day a week, every week of the year. Then counting, deposits, record-keeping and ordering supplies on other days. It's a real job, done with care and accountability.

What the law allows

A charity may pay customary and reasonable amounts for rent, supplies, advertising and security. It may not pay the people who actually run the game, and they may not accept pay from any source.

What happens to a real game

The Ohio Attorney General publishes every licensed bingo organization's numbers each year. Here is one veterans post in Delaware County. Its traditional game, the one that needs a crew, shut down. Its electronic instant bingo machines, which need no crew, grew past $6 million a year.

In the same years the post's electronic instant bingo receipts were about $5.6 million, $6.0 million and $6.0 million. The money didn't disappear. The community game did.

Source: Ohio Attorney General, Bingo Financials, fiscal years 2022 to 2025. Ohio's bingo fiscal year runs November 1 to October 31.

The rules we want changed

These are current Ohio law. Each one was written to protect charitable money. Each one also keeps honest games from running. We want the money kept traceable and the games kept open, and these changes do both.

1. Volunteers only

The rule: No one who works a bingo session may be paid, by the charity or anyone else.

What it does: Only people who can afford hundreds of unpaid hours a year can run a game.

What we ask: Let charities pay session workers a reasonable hourly wage, capped as a share of receipts, paid through the charity's own payroll with W-2s and background checks, and reported in the yearly bingo financials.

ORC 2915.09

2. No paid outside help

The rule: A charity running traditional bingo may not pay consulting fees for any service related to its session.

What it does: A hall that needs a bookkeeper or compliance help to run things right can't hire one for the game.

What we ask: Allow paid professional help, such as bookkeeping and compliance, at customary and reasonable rates.

ORC 2915.09

3. Members and guests only

The rule: Instant bingo and electronic instant bingo at veterans and fraternal clubs may be sold only to members and their invited guests.

What it does: On paper, a neighbor can't walk in and play. In practice, guests get signed in at the door, so the rule mostly adds paperwork and keeps new people away.

What we ask: Let any adult play, with an age check at the door.

ORC 2915.13

4. Three sessions a week

The rule: Most organizations may hold no more than three bingo sessions in seven days, and no premises may host more than nine a week.

What it does: A hall with demand for more nights has to turn players away.

What we ask: Let demand set the schedule, within hours the law already allows.

ORC 2915.09

5. A fixed prize cap

The rule: No more than $6,000 in traditional bingo prizes per session.

What it does: The cap doesn't move with prices, so prizes shrink every year next to machines and other games.

What we ask: Raise the cap and tie it to inflation.

ORC 2915.09

6. A two-year wait

The rule: A charity must exist in Ohio for two years before it can get a bingo license.

What it does: A new nonprofit ready to run a game, with a hall ready to host it, has to wait two years anyway.

What we ask: Let a 501(c)(3) in good standing with the IRS and the Attorney General apply sooner.

ORC 2915.01

Bills to watch

These bills were introduced in the 136th General Assembly (2025 to 2026) and would change Ohio's charitable gaming law. None of them fixes the rules above. Bills move and stall, so open the bill page for where each one stands today.

  • House Bill 344 Would let 501(c)(3) charities offer electronic instant bingo at bars and restaurants, authorize video lottery terminals at retailers, tax electronic instant bingo, and widen the hours veterans and fraternal organizations may offer instant games.
  • House Bill 476 Would authorize online raffles under the Charitable Gaming Law, on platforms approved by the Attorney General.
  • Senate Bill 197 A broad gambling bill. Among much else, it would move regulation of charitable bingo from the Attorney General to the Ohio Casino Control Commission on January 1, 2027.
  • Senate Bill 399 Would modify the Attorney General's authority under the Charitable Gaming Law.

Summaries are drawn from the bill titles and the Legislative Service Commission's analyses.

The letter

Dear [Representative or Senator] [Name],

I live in [your town], and I [play bingo / volunteer at a bingo game / run a bingo game] at [hall name]. I'm asking you to support changing Ohio Revised Code Chapter 2915 so charities can pay the people who run their bingo games.

Today, a charity may pay for rent, supplies, advertising and security, but not for the people who actually run the game. A weekly session takes about six hours, plus counting, deposits, records and ordering on other days. Because that work must be unpaid, traditional bingo survives only where there are retirees who can give it hundreds of hours a year. Elsewhere it closes, and the seniors and veterans who depend on a weekly game lose it.

Please support letting charities pay session workers a reasonable, capped hourly wage through their own payroll, with W-2 reporting, background checks, and the pay shown in the yearly bingo financials. That keeps every dollar traceable and keeps community games open.

[One or two sentences about your own game.]

Thank you for your time.

[Your name]
